Part I

Foundations

How Spanish works, how to say it, and how to start thinking in it — before we touch a single verb chart.

1 · How Spanish Works

Spanish is more regular than English. Once you learn a pattern, it repeats almost everywhere. Three big ideas will carry you a long way:

The three pillars
  • Gender. Every noun is masculine or feminine. el mar (the sea, m.), la playa (the beach, f.). The word for “the” changes to match.
  • Agreement. Adjectives copy the noun's gender and number: el perro cariñoso, las flores coloridas.
  • Verb endings carry the “who.” The ending of a verb already tells you who does it, so you can often drop the pronoun: hablo = I speak. No “yo” needed.

Gender in one minute

Most words ending in -o are masculine, most ending in -a are feminine. Exceptions exist (el día, la mano), but the rule wins the vast majority of the time.

3 · Accent Marks

That little mark (´) does two simple jobs:

  • It tells you where to stress the word. café = ca-FÉ. Without the mark you'd say CA-fe.
  • It separates look-alike words. sí (yes) vs si (if); tú (you) vs tu (your); él (he) vs el (the).
The stress rule (no accent needed)
  • Word ends in a vowel, n, or s → stress the second-to-last syllable. playa, hablan, perros.
  • Word ends in any other consonant → stress the last syllable. hablar, feliz, jardín (jardín breaks the rule, so it gets an accent).
  • An accent mark overrides all of this and just says “stress here.”

The upside-down ¿ and ¡ open questions and exclamations so you know the tone before you start reading: ¿Cómo estás? · ¡Qué rico!

4 · Rolling Your R's

There are two R sounds, and the difference actually matters:

  • Soft R (single r in the middle of a word): a quick tap of the tongue, like the tt in American “butter.” pero (but), mar, cariño.
  • Rolled RR (double rr, or r at the start of a word): a trill, several taps in a row. perro (dog), arroz (rice), remar (to paddle).
How to learn the trill
  • Say “butter-butter-butter” fast in an American accent — that tongue bounce is the soft R.
  • For the roll, relax your tongue and blow air so the tip flutters against the ridge behind your top teeth. Practice the phrase “erre con erre, cigarro”.
  • It takes weeks, not minutes. If you can't roll yet, use a soft tap — people will still understand you completely.
  • Watch out: pero (but) vs perro (dog) is your daily reminder to practice.

5 · Sentence Structure

Basic Spanish word order is the same as English — Subject → Verb → Object — so you can start building sentences immediately. A few twists:

  • 01Jenna bebe café.Jenna drinks coffee. (same order as English)
  • 02El perro cariñoso.The affectionate dog. (adjective comes AFTER the noun)
  • 03No quiero café.I don't want coffee. ('no' goes right before the verb)
  • 04¿Quieres café?Do you want coffee? (no 'do' — just raise your voice)
  • 05Me gusta la playa.I like the beach. (lit. 'the beach pleases me')
Two shortcuts you'll use constantly
  • Making it negative: just put no before the verb. That's the whole rule.
  • Asking a yes/no question: keep the same words, add ¿ ?, and let your voice rise at the end.

6 · Thinking in Spanish

The goal isn't to translate faster — it's to stop translating. A few habits make that happen sooner:

  • Name things in your day. Walking the dogs? Think los perros, la correa, la playa. You already live in Spanish-able moments.
  • Learn chunks, not words. Store ¿Cómo estás? as one piece, not three.
  • Talk to yourself. Narrate small actions: Voy a la cocina. Hago café.
  • Accept “baby Spanish.” Yo querer café is wrong but understandable. Speaking wrong beats staying silent — you fix it by using it.

8 · Memory Tricks That Actually Work

  • Hook new words to your life. la arena (sand) → picture the “arena” of your beach. el pollo (chicken) → a chicken saying “Polly.”
  • Group by the “g” yo-verbs. tengo, vengo, pongo, salgo, hago, digo, oigo — nearly all your irregular yo forms grow a G. Learn the club, not seven exceptions.
  • Say it out loud 3×. Muscle memory beats staring.
  • Spaced repetition. Use the flashcards in this pack: review a word today, tomorrow, in three days, then weekly.

Present Tense · Verb 1 of 16

Ser

to be (permanent — who/what something is)

When it's used

  • Who or what someone is: Soy Jenna. — I am Jenna.
  • Where someone is from: Él es de Cuba. — He is from Cuba.
  • Permanent traits: El café es fuerte. — The coffee is strong.
  • Telling time & dates: Son las ocho. — It's eight o'clock.
Memory trick

SER = the essence. If it's basically always true — your name, where you're from, what something is made of — use ser. Think "SER = the Story of who you are."

Completely irregular — memorize it as a little song: soy, eres, es… somos, sois, son.

Present Tense · Verb 4 of 16

Tener

to have

When it's used

  • Possession: Tengo dos perros. — I have two dogs.
  • Age: Tengo treinta años. — I'm thirty (lit. I have 30 years).
  • Feelings/states — tener idioms: Tengo hambre / sed / frío / calor / sueño / miedo / prisa.

Memory trick

TENER stem changes e→ie (tiene) but the yo form grows a 'g': tengo. Remember "I've GOT it" → tengo. And in Spanish you don't are hungry, you have hunger.

Part III

The Everyday Verb Forms

Part II gave you the present. This part gives you the future (two ways), the -ing form, reflexives, and stem-changers — each takes about ten minutes. The best news is hidden in all of them: only the first verb ever changes.

1 · The four things a verb can do

Same verb (trabajar, to work), four forms. Two you know, two are new:

EspañolEnglishNote
Trabajo hoy.I work today.present
Voy a trabajar mañana.I'm going to work tomorrow.near future — NEW
Estoy trabajando.I'm working (right now).progressive — NEW
Trabajé ayer.I worked yesterday.past
The rule that ties them together

In voy a trabajar, only voy is conjugated — trabajar stays in its plain dictionary form. Learn five “openers” and you can say hundreds of things while only ever changing these five:

quiero…I want to…puedo…I can…necesito…I need to…voy a…I'm going to…me gusta…I like to…tengo que…I have to…

2 · The near future — ir a + verb

This is how people actually talk about the future. Three pieces, and only the first one changes. The a never changes.

Going to + verb

túvas a…
él / ella / ustedva a…
nosotrosvamos a…
ellos / ellas / ustedesvan a…
Careful with “to the”

a + el = al. So Voy al gimnasio — not “a el.” With feminine words it stays apart: Voy a la playa.

  • 02Voy a hacer paddleboard con Joshua.I'm going to paddleboard with Joshua.
  • 03Mañana voy a regar las plantas.Tomorrow I'm going to water the plants.
  • 04Vamos a cenar con su familia.We're going to have dinner with his family.
  • 05Voy al gimnasio después del trabajo.I'm going to the gym after work.
  • 06¿Vas a estudiar español esta noche?Are you going to study Spanish tonight?

Don't overuse it

English says “I'm working tomorrow” for plans — Spanish does not. The progressive is strictly for right now. For tomorrow, use voy a trabajar.

  • 01Estoy aprendiendo español.I'm learning Spanish. (your most useful sentence!)
  • 02Estoy cocinando arroz con pollo.I'm cooking chicken and rice.

  • 04Lo estoy intentando.I'm trying it. (say this to yourself often!)
  • 05Estamos caminando por la playa.We're walking on the beach.
The little word “lo” (it)

It goes before estar: Lo estoy aprendiendo (I'm learning it) · Lo estoy haciendo (I'm doing it).

5 · Stem-changing verbs (the “boot”)

Some verbs change their middle vowel — but only in the present, and only in four of the five forms. Draw a line around the changed forms and you get a boot shape: nosotros is the one left outside.

o → ue · poder (can)

yopuedo
túpuedes
él / ella / ustedpuede
nosotrospodemos
ellos / ellas / ustedespueden

e → ie · querer (want)

yoquiero
túquieres
él / ella / ustedquiere
nosotrosqueremos
ellos / ellas / ustedesquieren

See how podemos and queremos keep the plain vowel? That's nosotros standing outside the boot.

The three changes
  • o → ue: poder→puedo, dormir→duermo, volver→vuelvo, costar→cuesta
  • e → ie: querer→quiero, pensar→pienso, empezar→empiezo, entender→entiendo
  • e → i: pedir→pido, servir→sirvo, repetir→repito

Watch out for the sneaky one: regar → riego (to water) is a stem-changer too — “I water the plants” is Riego las plantas, not “rego.”

In the past, the boot disappears

None of this happens in the preterite: volví, quise, entendí. The stem goes back to normal. That's why the past felt easier — it is.

  • 01Quiero aprender a cocinar comida cubana.I want to learn to cook Cuban food.
  • 02No puedo dormir cuando hace calor.I can't sleep when it's hot.
  • 03Riego las plantas todos los días.I water the plants every day.
  • 04Empiezo a trabajar a las nueve.I start work at nine.
  • 05Prefiero la playa por la mañana.I prefer the beach in the morning.

6 · The “real” future — hablaré

Day to day you'll mostly say voy a + verb. But you'll hear the one-word future too, and here's the secret nobody tells you: it's the easiest tense in Spanish. You don't chop anything — take the whole verb and stick the ending on. And there's only one set of endings for -ar, -er, and -ir.

hablar → will speak

yohablaré
túhablarás
él / ella / ustedhablará
nosotroshablaremos
ellos / ellas / ustedeshablarán
Same endings for every single verb

comeré (I'll eat) · viviré (I'll live) · iré (I'll go) · aprenderé (I'll learn). Whole verb + é. That's the entire lesson.

  • 01Mañana hablaré con su abuela.Tomorrow I'll talk with his grandma.
  • 02Este fin de semana iremos a la playa.This weekend we'll go to the beach.
  • 03Aprenderé español, poco a poco.I'll learn Spanish, little by little.
  • 04Joshua cocinará arroz con pollo el domingo.Joshua will cook chicken and rice on Sunday.
Which future should I use?

Speaking? Use voy a + verb — it's what people actually say. The -é endings are mostly for recognizing the future when you hear or read it.

3 · Pronunciation — How Cuban Sounds

You don't need to imitate it, but recognizing these patterns is the difference between understanding your boyfriend's family and nodding politely:

  • Dropped S. The s at the end of syllables fades: ¿Cómo estás? → “¿Cómo etá?”; más o menos → “má o meno.”
  • Swallowed D. The -ado ending softens: pescado → “pescao,” cansado → “cansao.”
  • R → L (in some areas). por favor can sound like “pol favol.”
  • Fast and musical. Words run together. Listen for the chunks, not every letter.
Train your ear

Search YouTube for “Cuban Spanish conversation” or listen to Cuban salsa and reggaeton with the lyrics up. Your goal isn't to catch every word — it's to get comfortable with the rhythm.

Usted everywhere

Colombians use usted with almost everyone — even friends, even family, even pets. Don't read it as cold; it's just warm politeness. So you may hear ¿Cómo está? where a Cuban would say ¿Cómo estás?

Neither one uses vosotros

Cuban and Colombian Spanish never use vosotros — that's why this whole workbook skips it. If a textbook makes you drill it, skip that page with a clear conscience.

5 · Culture Notes

  • Warmth is default. mi amor, mi vida, cariño are sprinkled everywhere — even the lady at the counter may call you “mi amor.”
  • Coffee is connection. A cafecito is an invitation, a pause, a sign of welcome. Never say no to one.
  • Family and food are the center. Expect big meals, loud tables, and lots of “come más” (eat more!). Complimenting the cook is always right.
  • Music is everywhere. Salsa, son, timba, reggaetón — and dancing isn't optional at a party. It's okay to be a beginner; joining in matters more than getting it right.
